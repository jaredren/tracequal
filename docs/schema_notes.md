# Decision Matrix Schema: Notes and Rationale (v1)

This document explains each column of TraceQual's decision matrix and the methods literature that motivates it. It is intended for two audiences: the researcher using the tool (who needs to know what they're being asked to document and why) and reviewers or instructors evaluating AI-assisted qualitative work produced with the tool.

## Schema philosophy

The schema is built on three commitments. First, the tool documents researcher decisions, it does not evaluate them. A reviewer reading the matrix should be able to assess methodological rigor, but the matrix itself takes no stance on whether the researcher chose well. Second, the schema is descriptive of an existing practice, not prescriptive of a new one. Researchers already make these decisions; the tool just makes them visible. Third, the schema is opinionated about granularity. One row per substantive AI suggestion, not one row per turn or one row per session, because that grain is where the methodological action is.

## Theoretical anchors

Three pieces of methods literature anchor the schema.

Feuston and Brubaker (2021) found that the stage of qualitative analysis matters for how scholars believe AI can and should be used, based on interviews with 15 CSCW and HCI qualitative researchers. This is the basis for the `analytic_stage` column. Their core insight, that AI use is not a single thing but a set of stage-specific practices with different stakes, is what makes a per-decision schema necessary rather than a session-level disclosure.

McDonald, Schoenebeck, and Forte (2019) argued that reliability practices in CSCW and HCI qualitative work vary widely and that inter-rater reliability is rare, occurring in around one in nine qualitative papers in their meta-analysis of CSCW and HCI papers from 2016-2018. Their framing matters here because a disclosure scaffold has to assume heterogeneous reliability practices. The schema deliberately does not require IRR-style agreement reporting; instead, it produces an audit trail that lets a reader assess reliability on their own terms.

Braun and Clarke's reflexive thematic analysis provides the six phases used in the `analytic_stage` column. Their framework includes (1) familiarization with data, (2) generating initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes, and (6) writing the report. The schema uses these as a closed enum rather than letting the extractor invent stage labels, because the goal is interpretability across studies, not novelty.

## Column-by-column rationale

### `turn_id` (integer)

Sequential index of the turn within the chat session. Not a timestamp, because timestamps are often missing from pasted transcripts and because turn order is what matters analytically. Two suggestions made twenty minutes apart but two turns apart are analytically adjacent; the time gap matters less than the conversational proximity.

### `timestamp` (ISO 8601 string or null)

Captured if the export format includes it (Claude's JSON export does, raw paste does not). Nullable. Used for the positioning pass to detect long pauses, which sometimes indicate the researcher stepped away to think. Not used for ordering; that's what `turn_id` is for.

### `researcher_prompt_summary` (string)

A one-sentence summary of what the researcher asked. Generated by the LLM extractor with a neutrality constraint in the prompt (no evaluative language, no inference about motivation). This is included rather than the raw prompt for three reasons: raw prompts can be very long, raw prompts often contain participant data that should not be re-circulated in an appendix, and summaries are easier to scan when reviewing a 60-row matrix.

A known limitation: summaries lose nuance. The methods reflection document discusses this and recommends researchers keep the original transcript alongside the matrix for any consequential audit.

### `ai_suggestion_summary` (string)

One-sentence neutral summary of what the AI proposed. Same constraints and same limitations as the prompt summary. The pairing of these two columns is what makes a row interpretable: a reader can see what was asked, what was offered, and what was done about it.

### `decision` (enum: accepted, modified, rejected, deferred, unclear)

The core column. Five values, chosen to be exhaustive and minimally overlapping.

`accepted` means the researcher used the AI's suggestion substantially as-given. Light editing (formatting, grammar) does not count as modification. `modified` means the researcher took the suggestion's substance but changed it in a way that affects analytic meaning (rewording a code, narrowing a theme, splitting one suggestion into two). `rejected` means the researcher explicitly did not use the suggestion, either by saying so or by continuing in a contradictory direction. `deferred` means the researcher set the suggestion aside for later consideration without accepting or rejecting it. `unclear` is the honest fallback when the transcript does not show what the researcher did next.

The `deferred` category is included specifically because Feuston and Brubaker's work shows that qualitative researchers often treat AI as a source of ideas to revisit rather than immediate inputs. Collapsing `deferred` into `accepted` or `rejected` would erase that practice.

### `reasoning` (string)

The researcher's stated or inferred rationale for the decision. The schema prefers stated reasoning. When reasoning is inferred from context, the extractor prefixes the field with `[inferred]`. This convention is non-negotiable: it lets a reader immediately distinguish what the researcher said from what the tool guessed.

This column is where the methodological value is concentrated. A matrix with full `decision` columns but empty `reasoning` columns is a list of behaviors; with `reasoning` filled, it is a record of judgment.

### `decision_stated` (boolean)

`True` when the transcript directly states what the researcher did with the AI suggestion (`accepted`, `modified`, `rejected`, `deferred`, `unclear`). `False` when that decision is inferred from surrounding turns. This flag separates transcript evidence from extractor inference and is the primary input to confidence derivation.

### `reasoning_stated` (boolean)

`True` when the transcript directly states why the researcher responded as they did. `False` when rationale is inferred from context. When `False`, the `reasoning` field must retain the `[inferred]` prefix.

### `analytic_stage` (enum: familiarization, coding, theming, reviewing, defining, writeup)

Maps to Braun and Clarke's six phases. The extractor uses `coding` as the default when ambiguous, because that is the phase where AI assistance is most commonly engaged in current practice and the safest no-information prior. A researcher conducting deductive analysis with a pre-built codebook can ignore the `familiarization` and `theming` values; they will rarely fire.

The schema does not distinguish between Braun and Clarke's reflexive thematic analysis and other thematic approaches, because the phase labels travel reasonably well across approaches. The methods reflection notes this limitation for researchers using grounded theory, framework analysis, or interpretive phenomenological analysis, where the phase names will fit less cleanly.

### `confidence` (enum: high, medium, low)

`Confidence` is rule-derived, not self-reported by the model. The extractor records two booleans on each row: `decision_stated` and `reasoning_stated`, then computes confidence using a fixed mapping:

- `high`: `decision_stated = true` and `reasoning_stated = true`
- `medium`: `decision_stated = true` and `reasoning_stated = false`
- `low`: `decision_stated = false` (regardless of `reasoning_stated`)

This mapping is intentionally inspectable so reviewers can audit whether confidence values reflect transcript evidence rather than model self-assessment.

#### Observed distribution and its limitation

On the committed canonical cache (`outputs/synthetic_long_decisions__prompt-1.0.0.json`, 23 rows, schema v1.0.0), model-reported confidence distributed as `high=19`, `medium=3`, `low=1`, skewing high rather than surfacing uncertainty. After schema v1.1.0 replaced self-report with rule-derived confidence from `decision_stated` and `reasoning_stated`, a subsequent extraction on the same fixture (22 rows, `outputs/synthetic_long_decisions__prompt-1.2.0.json`) distributed as `high=5`, `medium=14`, `low=3`, meaning roughly two-thirds of rows fell into `medium`. That derived skew follows directly from the rule: `medium` is assigned when the decision is explicitly stated but the reasoning is inferred, and in coding sessions researchers often state what they will do while leaving why implicit. This is therefore a finding about the data-generating interaction pattern, not a bug in implementation: a reasoning-grounded confidence signal has limited discriminating power when decisions are externalized more explicitly than reasons. The v1 rule is locked; redesign is deferred to future work.

Researchers should hand-check every `low` confidence row before exporting. The notebook surfaces these rows separately for that purpose.

## Secondary table: `positioning_log`

A four-column companion table for the epistemic positioning pass.

| Column | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `turn_id` | int | Anchor in the main matrix |
| `shift_type` | enum | `broadening`, `narrowing`, `reframing`, `reaffirming`, `destabilizing` |
| `evidence_quote` | string | Verbatim excerpt (under 15 words) from the researcher's turn |
| `note` | string | One-sentence interpretation of the shift |

The five shift types are drawn from reflexive thematic analysis literature on how researchers' interpretive frames evolve during coding. `Broadening` and `narrowing` are scope shifts. `Reframing` is a change in the conceptual lens. `Reaffirming` is when the researcher pushed back on an AI suggestion in a way that strengthened their existing frame. `Destabilizing` is when the AI suggestion produced genuine uncertainty about a prior commitment.

The positioning log is sparse by design. A typical session produces five to ten rows in the main matrix per ten rows of researcher turns, but only one to three rows in the positioning log. Most decisions are local; shifts are rare and worth noting precisely because they are rare.

## What the schema does not capture

Three things, flagged explicitly so reviewers know.

First, it does not capture power dynamics or institutional context. A graduate student using AI under advisor pressure is not analytically distinguishable in the matrix from a faculty researcher using AI freely. That information has to live in the methods section prose, not the matrix.

Second, it does not capture the AI's confidence or hedging. The matrix records what the AI suggested, not how strongly it suggested it. This is a deliberate choice: capturing AI hedging would expand the schema significantly and would tempt readers to treat AI confidence as a signal of suggestion quality, which it is not.

Third, it does not capture cumulative effects. Each row is local. If a researcher accepted twelve AI-suggested codes in a row without much modification, that pattern is visible by reading the matrix but is not flagged as a row. The positioning log is the partial answer to this, but it catches stance shifts, not cumulative absorption.

## Versioning

This is schema v1.1. Changes to columns, enums, or semantics require a version bump and an entry in `schema_changelog.md`. Backward compatibility is not guaranteed across major versions; the notebook reads the schema version from the JSON file and refuses to process matrices with mismatched versions.
